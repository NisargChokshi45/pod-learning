# M8.2 — Verification: hallucination detection, output validation, code verification

| Status | Audience | Hours | Prerequisite |
|---|---|---|---|
| CORE · **cannot be exempted** | All clusters | 1.5 | Any two D3 modules. In this pack's teaching order those are M3.2 and M3.4 |

**Objective:** catch wrong, invented or unsafe output **before** it reaches a client, a codebase or a decision.

> At Foundational, someone reviewed your output. At Practitioner, **you are that reviewer**. This module
> is the habit the whole level depends on: assume fluent output is unchecked until you've checked it, and
> spend checking effort in proportion to what an error would cost.

---

## TL;DR

- **Models err in five recognisable ways:** fabrication, over-generalisation, stale knowledge, instruction
  drift and sycophancy. **A confident tone tells you nothing about correctness.** Fluency is the default,
  not a signal.
- **The high-risk content types:** citations, numbers, legal and regulatory statements, API and library names,
  versions, people and dates. Check these first, always.
- **Check the quote, not the claim.** Ask for sources and quotes, then confirm that the quote *exists* in the
  source and *says what was claimed*. Cross-check against search, the source document or the system of record.
- **Validate by output type:** text gets a fact table and a source check, data gets independent recomputation,
  code gets run, tested and its dependencies checked, analysis gets its assumptions challenged, and decisions
  get "what would have to be true".
- **Verification prompts help.** These are a rubric self-check, a second opinion in a fresh chat, an
  adversarial review and "what are you least sure about". But **a self-check is a filter, not a guarantee.**
- **Be proportional.** Sample low-risk bulk work. Do a full review of anything **external, financial, legal,
  security-relevant or production-bound**.
- **Record it.** Every deliverable gets a **verification note** (what was checked, how, by whom) and a
  **named human** who signs it off, every time.

## Concepts

### 1. Why models err

| Cause | Mechanism (one level down) | Typical symptom |
|---|---|---|
| **Fabrication** | The model generates the most plausible continuation. When the true detail isn't in its context or training, a plausible-looking one is still the likeliest text | A real-looking citation, function or clause number that doesn't exist |
| **Over-generalisation** | It learned patterns across many sources and applies the common case to your specific one | "Postgres does X" when your managed service disables X; "UK law says" applied to an Irish contract |
| **Stale knowledge** | Training stops at a cutoff, and products, versions, prices and laws keep moving | A deprecated model ID, an old API parameter, last year's rate limit |
| **Instruction drift** | In long contexts, early instructions get less weight than recent content (M2.3) | Constraints from turn 2 are ignored at turn 40 |
| **Sycophancy** | Training rewards answers people approve of, so it leans towards your framing | It "confirms" the root cause you suggested; it flips when you push back |

**Why a confident tone carries no information about correctness.** The model produces fluent, assured text
whether or not the content is right. Generation optimises for plausible text, and plausible text sounds
sure. Unlike a human expert, there's no reliable link between how certain it *sounds* and how likely it is
to be right. So you can't use tone as a filter. Every high-risk claim gets checked on its merits, and a
hedge ("I believe…") doesn't mean the claim is wrong.

### 2. Hallucination detection

**The high-risk content types.** These are where fabrication and staleness concentrate, and where an error
does the most damage:

| Type | Why it's high-risk | Check against |
|---|---|---|
| **Citations** (papers, articles, URLs, case law) | Easy to fabricate convincingly: plausible author, title and year | Open the source. Does it exist, and does it say that? |
| **Numbers** (figures, percentages, totals, limits) | Arithmetic slips; invented statistics | Recompute; trace to the input or system of record |
| **Legal and regulatory statements** | Over-generalised across jurisdictions; stale | The statute or regulation text, or your legal team |
| **API and library names** | Plausible-but-nonexistent functions, flags or packages | Official docs; actually import or call it |
| **Versions** | Stale knowledge, so the latest version is wrong | Release notes, changelog, package registry |
| **People** (names, roles, quotes) | Invented attributions; wrong roles | Org chart, the original source |
| **Dates** | Stale or invented; timezone slips | The system of record; the original thread |

**Ask for sources and quotes, then check two things:**

1. **The quote exists.** Search the source document for the exact text (Ctrl+F) or a distinctive
   fragment of it. Paraphrased "quotes" are a red flag.
2. **It says what was claimed.** A real quote can still be misused. It might be out of context, about a
   different product tier, or a "may" turned into a "will". Read the surrounding paragraph.

```text
For every factual claim in your answer, give the supporting quote from the provided documents
in a table: claim | exact quote | document | page/section. If no quote supports a claim, write
"no source" in the quote column instead of paraphrasing.
```

**Cross-check against an independent source:**

- **Search:** confirms that public facts exist and are current. But search is retrieval, not
  verification (M3.4). A search result can be wrong too.
- **The source document:** for anything Claude extracted or summarised from a document.
- **The system of record:** for internal facts like the CRM, the HR system, the ticket tracker or the
  billing system. A Claude summary of the CRM isn't the CRM.

### 3. Validation by output type

| Output type | Validation method | Concretely |
|---|---|---|
| **Text** | A fact table and a source check | Extract every factual claim into a table (claim, source, verified Y/N), then check the high-risk rows first |
| **Data** | **Recompute independently** | Recompute the key figures in a different tool (Excel or Sheets, SQL, a script you wrote). Don't ask Claude to "double-check its maths" (M3.5) |
| **Code** | Run it, test it, check the dependencies | Does it compile and run? Do the tests pass, and do they assert anything meaningful (M5.5)? Do the imported packages and APIs exist, at those versions, under an approved licence (M8.3)? |
| **Analysis** | Challenge the assumptions | List the assumptions, and for each ask "what if this is false?" Try to find disconfirming evidence |
| **Decisions** | List what would have to be true | "For this recommendation to be right, X, Y and Z must hold." Then check X, Y and Z |

> **Forward reference:** the syllabus says "the full process is M5.4" for code. **M5.4 (review and secure coding)
> is an Advanced-level module.** At Practitioner, the minimum for code is: it runs, its tests pass and
> fail for the right reasons, every dependency exists and is pinned, and a human has read the diff.

### 4. Verification prompts

| Prompt | What it catches | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| **Rubric self-check**: "score this against the rubric; fix failures" | Format breaches, missed requirements, internal inconsistency | Wrong facts the model believes are right |
| **Second-opinion pass in a fresh chat**: paste only the output plus a reviewer role | Errors the original chat was committed to; framing it had already accepted | Shared blind spots. It's still the same model and training |
| **Adversarial review**: "find the strongest objections / the three most likely errors" | Weak reasoning, unsupported leaps, missing alternatives | Errors in facts it can't check |
| **"What are you least sure about?"** | It surfaces low-confidence claims for you to check first | Confident errors, by definition |

**Why a self-check is a filter, not a guarantee.** The checker is the same model with the same knowledge
and blind spots. It reliably finds *slips* (an arithmetic error, a dropped requirement) because it can see
them on rereading. It can't find a *belief* error (a fabricated API it thinks exists) because, to it, the
claim looks right. So use verification prompts to **narrow** where you look, then do an **external**
check (a source, a recomputation, a test run, the system of record). The prompt is triage. The external
check is the verification.

### 5. Proportional verification

Verification costs time, so spend it where an error would cost the most.

| Risk | Examples | Verification level |
|---|---|---|
| **Low**: internal, reversible, low-stakes, bulk | Tagging 2,000 support tickets; first-draft internal notes; brainstorming | **Sample**: check a random sample (for example 20–30 items), track the error rate, and escalate to full review if it exceeds your threshold |
| **Medium**: internal decisions, team-facing documents | A sprint retro summary; an internal design note | Check the high-risk content types and the key claims |
| **High**: external, financial, legal, security-relevant, production-bound | A client proposal with figures; a compliance answer; a production code change; an executive summary used for a decision | **Full review**: every claim, every figure, every dependency, with a named human signing off |

The five triggers for full review are **external, financial, legal, security-relevant and
production-bound**. If any one applies, do a full review, however small the deliverable. A two-line
client email quoting a price is "external + financial".

At Practitioner level this should happen **without being prompted**. Nobody will remind you to recompute
the figure before the deck goes out.

### 6. Recording verification

**The verification note** goes on (or with) every deliverable above low risk:

```markdown
## Verification note
**Deliverable:** Q3 vendor comparison for ACME, v2 · **Owner (sign-off):** Priya N. · **Date:** 2026-11-20
**AI use:** Claude (Research mode + chat) drafted sections 2–4 and the comparison table.

| What was checked | How | By | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14 cited sources | Opened each; confirmed quote & date | Priya | 13 OK; 1 fabricated, removed |
| Cost table (3 vendors × 4 lines) | Recomputed in Sheets from price pages | Sam | 1 slip fixed (annual vs monthly) |
| Regulatory claim (data residency) | Checked against vendor DPA §5 | Priya | OK |
| Not checked | Vendor roadmap claims (not verifiable; marked "vendor-stated") | — | — |
```

What makes a note useful: **what** was checked, **how** (the method, not "reviewed"), **by whom**, the
**result including the errors found**, and **what was not checked**. A note that lists the errors found and
fixed is more credible than "all verified".

**Sign-off ownership:** a **named human**, every time. Not "the team", not "AI-assisted, reviewed". The
person who signs owns the content as if they'd written it (M8.3).

## Under the hood

### Why fabrication looks real

A language model learns the *shape* of a citation (author list, year, title pattern, venue) separately
from any particular true citation. When the context asks for a supporting reference and none is available,
the most likely text is a well-formed one. That's why fabricated references are **well-formatted, plausible
and specific**: specificity is part of the learned shape. Tools change this. With web search or a provided
document, the model can copy real text instead of generating it, which is why **grounding plus
quote-checking** works and "please don't hallucinate" doesn't.

### Grounding vs. verification

| | Grounding | Verification |
|---|---|---|
| **When** | Before and during generation | After generation |
| **What** | Gives the model the right material: documents, search, connectors | Checks the output against an independent source |
| **Reduces** | Fabrication and staleness | Everything, including grounding failures (a misread quote, a wrong document) |
| **Examples** | "Answer only from the provided documents" (M2.3); Research mode (M3.4) | Quote check, recomputation, test run, system-of-record lookup |

You need both. A grounded answer can still misquote, cite the wrong page, or apply a correct quote to the
wrong case.

### Citations on the API make quote-checking mechanical [api]

On the API, enabling citations on document blocks returns the exact cited span with its location (a
character range for text, a page range for PDFs). Your code can then confirm that each cited span really
appears in the source, with no trust in the model's paraphrase:

```python
import anthropic

client = anthropic.Anthropic()  # reads ANTHROPIC_API_KEY from the environment
policy = open("travel-policy.txt").read()  # internal doc, cleared for use (M8.1)

resp = client.messages.create(
    model="claude-opus-5-5",
    max_tokens=4000,
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": [
        {"type": "document",
         "source": {"type": "text", "media_type": "text/plain", "data": policy},
         "title": "Travel policy v7",
         "citations": {"enabled": True}},
        {"type": "text", "text": "What is the hotel cap for London, and who approves exceptions?"},
    ]}],
)

for block in resp.content:
    if block.type == "text" and getattr(block, "citations", None):
        for c in block.citations:
            # Mechanical check: the cited span must appear verbatim in the source.
            assert c.cited_text in policy, f"cited text not found: {c.cited_text!r}"
            print(f"OK  chars {c.start_char_index}-{c.end_char_index}: {c.cited_text[:60]}…")
```

The check proves the quote **exists**. A human still checks that it **supports the claim**. Citations
can't be combined with structured outputs (`output_config.format`) on the same request [api]. M7.1 covers
this.

### Second-opinion pass: fresh context [api]

The "fresh chat" principle as code. It's a separate request containing only the artefact and a reviewer
brief, with none of the history that produced the draft:

```python
draft = open("exec-summary-draft.md").read()

review = client.messages.create(
    model="claude-opus-5-5",
    max_tokens=8000,
    output_config={"effort": "high"},
    system="You are a sceptical reviewer. You did not write this document.",
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": (
        f"<document>\n{draft}\n</document>\n\n"
        "1) List every number, date, name, citation and product/version claim as a table: "
        "claim | type | what would verify it.\n"
        "2) List the five claims most likely to be wrong, most likely first, with your reason.\n"
        "Do not rewrite the document."
    )}],
)
```

Its output is a **checklist for you**, ordered by risk. It isn't a verdict.

### Sampling maths for bulk work (just enough)

If you check `n` randomly sampled items and find `k` errors, the error rate is roughly `k/n`. But a small
sample is uncertain: 0 errors in 20 items is consistent with a true error rate as high as ~14% (the "rule
of three": 3/n is the approximate 95% upper bound when you find none). So set the sample size from the
error rate you can tolerate, and **stratify** (sample from each category, not just the common one). If the
cost of a single miss is high, you're not in sampling territory. Do a full review.

## In production

**1. Client proposal with figures**
*Situation:* Claude drafted a proposal with a 3-year TCO table and two industry statistics.
*Right move:* This is external + financial, so it gets a full review. Recompute the TCO table in a
spreadsheet. Open the sources for both statistics. Write a verification note and sign it.
*Failure mode:* checking that the prose reads well. One statistic turns out to be invented, and the client's
procurement team finds it.

**2. Generated code using an unfamiliar SDK**
*Situation:* Claude wrote an integration that calls `client.reports.export_async()` on a vendor SDK.
*Right move:* Check that the method exists at your pinned SDK version (the docs or the installed package),
run the code against a sandbox, and confirm the tests assert real behaviour. Check every new dependency's
licence (M8.3).
*Failure mode:* it "looks right" and passes a test that mocks the very call that doesn't exist.

**3. Compliance questionnaire answer**
*Situation:* A security questionnaire asks about encryption at rest and data retention.
*Right move:* This is legal + security-relevant + external. Answer only from your policy documents, with
quotes. Verify every quote. Have security sign off by name.
*Failure mode:* Claude over-generalises ("data is encrypted with AES-256 at rest") from common practice,
not from your actual architecture.

**4. Ticket-tagging run on 3,000 tickets**
*Situation:* Internal, reversible, used for a trend chart.
*Right move:* Sample about 40 tickets stratified by tag, record the error rate in the note, and escalate if it
exceeds 5%.
*Failure mode:* a full manual review (wasted effort), or no review (a mis-tagged category drives a wrong
roadmap decision).

**5. Executive summary of a long RCA**
*Situation:* A one-page summary of a 30-page incident review, going to the leadership team.
*Right move:* Build a fact table of every number, date, name and causal claim and trace each to the RCA. Run
a fresh-chat second opinion to find the risky claims fast. Name the sign-off owner.
*Failure mode:* the summary says "47 minutes of downtime". The RCA says 47 minutes of *degradation* and 12 of
downtime. That's a sycophantic smoothing of the story.

## Pitfalls

| Anti-pattern | Why it bites | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Trusting a confident, polished answer | Tone carries no correctness signal | Check the high-risk content types every time |
| "Are you sure?" in the same chat | Sycophancy: it may flip a correct answer or double down on a wrong one | External check, or a fresh-chat second opinion |
| Checking that a citation *exists* but not what it *says* | Real sources get misquoted or used out of context | Read the surrounding paragraph |
| Asking Claude to re-check its own arithmetic | Same model, same slip risk | Recompute in a different tool |
| Treating search results as verified | Search is retrieval; results can be wrong or stale | Prefer primary sources; reconcile disagreements (M3.4) |
| Same verification effort on everything | Under-checks high-stakes work, wastes time on bulk | Proportional: sample, key claims or full review |
| "Reviewed" with no detail | Nobody can tell what was checked | Verification note: what, how, by whom, result, not checked |
| "The team" or "AI" as owner | No accountability | A named human signs off |
| Tests that mock the thing being verified | A green suite proves nothing (M5.5) | Run against real or sandbox behaviour; read the assertions |

## Hands-on exercises

1. **Seeded-error hunt.** Find the six seeded errors in a Claude-written market summary, using search and the
   source documents.
   **Done when:** you've found at least 5 of 6 (80%+). For each one, you've recorded the location, the error
   type (from the high-risk list) and the source that disproves it.
2. **Recompute an analysis.** Validate a Claude-generated spreadsheet analysis by recomputing it in a
   second tool.
   **Done when:** every headline figure has been recomputed independently, and every discrepancy is listed
   with its cause (formula, filter, unit or rounding).
3. **Cluster-specific verification.** *Technical clusters:* verify 150 lines of generated code using the
   Practitioner minimum (it runs, tests are meaningful, dependencies exist and are licensed, the diff is
   read). M5.4's full checklist comes at Advanced. *Non-technical clusters:* verify a generated policy
   summary against the policy text.
   **Done when:** you have a findings list with line or section references, and each finding is classified
   (fabrication, over-generalisation, stale, drift, or omission).
4. **Real verification note.** Write a verification note for a real deliverable you're about to send.
   **Done when:** it names the owner, lists the method per check, includes at least one "not checked"
   line, and the owner has signed it.
