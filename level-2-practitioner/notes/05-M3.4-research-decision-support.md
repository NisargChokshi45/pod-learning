# M3.4 — Research and decision support

| Status | Audience | Hours | Prerequisite |
|---|---|---|---|
| CORE | All clusters | 1.5 | M2.2 (reasoning guidance and advanced prompting) |

**Objective:** gather, verify and structure information for a decision, and **never present an
unchecked source as fact**.

> Claude makes research fast. It does not make it true. A search finds pages, and a report summarises them.
> Neither checks whether the pages are right, current or even say what the summary claims. This module
> covers the parts that stay your job: scoping the question, checking the sources, and presenting
> trade-offs a decision-maker can act on.

---

## TL;DR

- **Search is retrieval, not verification** [app; web search has no GA/BETA label in the docs]. Claude found a page; that tells you nothing about
  whether the page is correct. Read what it searched and open the sources.
- **Research mode** [app, PLAN: Pro and above] runs multi-step research across the web and connected
  sources. It needs web search turned on. **The citations are the deliverable; the prose is a draft.**
- **Scope the question first:** decision, options, criteria, constraints, time horizon and what is
  out of scope. A vague question gets a vague report.
- **Evaluate sources:** primary over secondary, recent over old, and an explicit reconciliation when
  sources disagree. Check every citation: does the link resolve, does the quote exist, does it say what was claimed?
- **Decision support = options + weighted criteria + pre-mortem + assumption register.** Present
  **trade-offs and "what would have to be true"**, not a verdict.
- **Confidentiality:** never paste client strategy, deal terms or unannounced plans into a research prompt.
  Research the market, not your client's secrets.
- **The deliverable:** summary first, then an evidence table, open questions, and a sources list the reader can check.

## Concepts

### 1. Web search in chat

**When Claude searches on its own** [app; no status label in the docs]. With web search available, Claude searches when the question
needs current or external information it doesn't have: recent events, prices, versions, documentation.
In the newer Claude experience there is **no web-search toggle**; Claude searches when it judges it helps. On
Team and Enterprise, an **Owner must enable web search** for the organisation first [app, PLAN].

**How to force a search.** Say so explicitly: "Search the web for … and cite the sources", or name the
source ("check the vendor's current pricing page"). Also constrain it: "only official vendor documentation and
standards bodies; no blogs".

**Reading what it searched.** The reply shows the searches and the pages used. Before trusting the answer,
check three things. Were the queries sensible? Were the sources authoritative? Did the answer cite the
sources it actually used?

**Search is retrieval, not verification.** Search ranks pages by relevance, not truth. A confident SEO
article, an outdated forum answer and the vendor's own marketing all rank. Claude summarises
what it retrieved. If the retrieved page is wrong, the summary is fluently wrong.

### 2. Research mode

**What it is** [app, PLAN: Pro and above]. Multi-step research. Claude plans, runs many searches across the web and
**connected sources** (such as Drive or email), reads, follows up, and writes a cited report. It takes minutes,
not seconds.

**Requirements and behaviour** [app]:
- **Requires web search to be turned on.**
- **On mobile, connectors are skipped**: it searches only the web, silently. Run research that needs internal
  sources from web or desktop.
- **It runs on a fixed model, whatever model you selected.**

> **Syllabus says:** Research runs on Opus 5.5; Pro and above; requires web search; connectors
> skipped on mobile · **Current (verified 2026-11-28, support.claude.com/en/articles/11088861):**
> confirmed, and it **falls back to Opus 4.8**. Available on Pro, Max, Ultra, Team and Enterprise. On
> Team/Enterprise it won't run if an admin has disabled both models for your role.

**Scoping a research question so the report is usable.** Use a brief, not a question:

```text
Decision: whether to replace our self-hosted Kafka with a managed streaming service in 2027.
Options in scope: AWS MSK, Confluent Cloud, Google Pub/Sub. Out of scope: self-hosted alternatives.
Criteria: data residency in EU, exactly-once support, ops burden, 3-year cost at ~2 TB/day, exit cost.
Constraints: must run in eu-west-1 or eu-central; SOC 2 Type II required.
Sources: prefer official docs, pricing pages, and standards/regulator sites; flag anything older than 12 months.
Output: summary (≤150 words) → evidence table (claim | option | source | date) → open questions.
Do not recommend; present trade-offs.
```

**Reading the report critically.** The prose reads well whether it is right or not. Read it as a list of
claims:
1. Is every material claim cited?
2. Open the citations for every number, every compliance or regulatory claim, and every claim the decision
   turns on.
3. Check that the cited page says what the report claims (see *Under the hood: misattribution*).
4. Note which claims rest on a single source.

### 3. Source evaluation

| Dimension | Prefer | Be wary of |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary vs secondary** | Vendor docs, pricing pages, standards, regulator text, the actual paper | Blog summaries, listicles, "X vs Y" comparison sites (often affiliate-funded) |
| **Recency** | Dated pages within your decision's horizon | Undated pages; anything that predates a major version |
| **Independence** | Third-party benchmarks with method published | Vendor benchmarks of their own product |
| **Specificity** | Exact limits, versions, prices, dates | "Industry-leading", "up to" figures |

**Reconciling sources that disagree.** Don't average them. List the disagreement, check dates (the
newer one may supersede), check scope (different regions, editions, tiers), and prefer the primary source.
If it remains unresolved, it becomes an **open question** with an owner, not a footnote.

**Citation checking and fabricated references.** A citation can be wrong in three ways. It doesn't
exist. It exists but doesn't contain the claim. Or it contains the claim in a different context
(another product, an older version). Click every high-risk citation. The full detection technique is in M8.2.

### 4. Structured decision support

**Options analysis.** Use at least three options, including "do nothing / status quo" as an honest baseline.

**Weighted criteria.** Agree the criteria and weights with the decision-maker **before** scoring, because
weights set after scoring are a rationalisation. Then score on evidence, citing it.

Worked example (scores 1–5; weights sum to 100%):

| Criterion | Weight | Tool A | Tool B | Tool C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Security & compliance | 30% | 4 | 5 | 3 |
| 3-year cost | 25% | 3 | 2 | 5 |
| Integration fit | 20% | 5 | 3 | 4 |
| Ops burden | 15% | 3 | 4 | 4 |
| Vendor viability | 10% | 4 | 5 | 2 |
| **Weighted total** | | **3.80** | **3.70** | **3.75** |

Tool A: 0.30×4 + 0.25×3 + 0.20×5 + 0.15×3 + 0.10×4 = 1.20 + 0.75 + 1.00 + 0.45 + 0.40 = **3.80**.
Tool B: 1.50 + 0.50 + 0.60 + 0.60 + 0.50 = **3.70**. Tool C: 0.90 + 1.25 + 0.80 + 0.60 + 0.20 = **3.75**.

**Sensitivity check, which is the step people skip.** The three totals are within 0.10, which is a tie. Move 10
points from security to cost (20% / 35%): A = 0.80 + 1.05 + 1.00 + 0.45 + 0.40 = **3.70**; B = 1.00 + 0.70
+ 0.60 + 0.60 + 0.50 = **3.40**; C = 0.60 + 1.75 + 0.80 + 0.60 + 0.20 = **3.95**. The winner flips to C. What
you report is: *"the choice depends on how much you value security versus cost"*. That is a trade-off for the
decision-maker, not a verdict from the matrix. Ask Claude to compute the totals **with code** and to run
the sensitivity check (M3.5).

**Pre-mortem.** "It's 12 months later and this decision failed. Write the five most likely reasons." This
works well with Claude, because imagining failure is easier than predicting it, and it gets around the optimism of
a recommendation draft.

| # | Failure story | Likelihood | Impact | Early warning sign | Mitigation | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Egress costs triple the forecast | M | H | Month-2 bill > 1.5× model | Cap via architecture review | FinOps lead |

**Assumption register.** Every recommendation rests on assumptions. Write them down so they can be tested.

| # | Assumption | Evidence | Confidence | What changes if false | Test / owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Volume grows ≤20%/year | 3-year trend | Medium | Cost ranking flips to Tool C | Re-check at Q2 review / data lead |

**"What would have to be true for this to be the right call?"** Ask it for *each* option. It turns
argument into checkable conditions: "Tool B is right if security weighs more than 35% (at 35%, taken from cost, B ties A at 3.85) *and*
volume stays under 2 TB/day". The decision-maker can then judge the conditions.

**Presenting trade-offs instead of a verdict.** The decision belongs to a named human who owns the
consequences (M8.3). You can state a recommendation, but put the trade-offs, the sensitivity and the
conditions next to it.

### 5. Competitive, market and vendor research

**Comparison templates.** Use the same columns for every vendor (capabilities, limits, pricing model, compliance,
support, exit terms, source and date). This stops the report praising one vendor's strengths while
skipping another's.

**Confidentiality boundaries.** Research prompts often go to the open web through search queries, and
some content may be logged by third parties. Even inside your plan, the prompt is stored in your chat
history.
- Research **the market**: "managed streaming services with EU residency and exactly-once".
- **Don't paste client strategy**: "Northwind plans to drop LogPilot before its Q3 acquisition of
  FastShip". That puts unannounced deal information into a prompt, which is a breach of the client's
  confidentiality, whatever the plan terms say (M8.1).
- Anonymise: "a mid-size EU logistics company" instead of the client name.

### 6. Writing the research deliverable

**Skeleton:**

```markdown
# <Decision question>
**Decision owner:** <name> · **Prepared by:** <name> · **Date:** <date> · **Status:** draft for decision

## Summary (≤150 words)
What we looked at, the trade-off in one sentence, the conditions under which each option wins.

## Options and weighted comparison
<matrix + sensitivity note>

## Evidence table
| # | Claim | Option | Source (link) | Source date | Primary? | Checked by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## Risks (pre-mortem) and assumptions
<tables>

## Open questions
| Question | Why it matters | Owner | Due |

## Sources
Numbered list; every entry openable by the reader; retrieval date.
```

Put the summary first, because executives read the top. Put the evidence table next, because reviewers check it. Open questions
make the uncertainty explicit, and sources the reader can check make the work auditable.

## Under the hood

### How multi-step research works

```text
question ──► plan (sub-questions) ──► search ──► read ──► gaps? ──yes──► more searches
                                                     │
                                                     no
                                                     ▼
                                       synthesise ──► cite ──► report
```

Each loop is the model deciding what to look for next, based on what it has read so far. Two
consequences follow. Early sources shape later queries, so one wrong early page can steer the whole
investigation (anchoring). And synthesis compresses many pages into a few claims, which is where
attribution errors creep in.

### Why citations can still misattribute

The report is written *after* reading many pages. Each citation is linked to a claim during synthesis,
not copied mechanically from the page. Common failure modes:

| Failure | What happened | How you catch it |
|---|---|---|
| **Merged sources** | Claim combines facts from page 3 and page 7; cites page 3 | The cited page has only half the claim |
| **Context shift** | Page states the limit for the Enterprise tier; claim drops the tier | Read the sentence around the quote |
| **Stale page** | Correct when written, superseded since | Check the page date and the vendor's changelog |
| **Paraphrase drift** | "up to 99.99%" becomes "99.99% guaranteed" | Compare the exact wording |
| **Plausible but absent** | The page is relevant; the specific number isn't on it | Ctrl+F for the number |

A citation shows **where Claude says it got the claim**. It doesn't prove the claim. That's why
"the citations are the point". They make checking possible, and you still have to do the checking.

### Why research prose is persuasive regardless of quality

Fluency is a property of the language model, not of the evidence. A report built on three weak sources reads as
smoothly as one built on thirty strong ones. Confidence of tone carries **no information** about
correctness (M8.2). Judge the evidence table, not the prose.

### Weighted scoring is arithmetic on judgements

The totals look precise to two decimals, but the inputs are 1–5 judgements and negotiated weights. That's
why ties within about 0.2 should be reported as ties, and why the sensitivity check matters more than the
total.

## In production

**1. Technology evaluation**
*Situation:* The platform team must choose a feature-flag service.
*Right move:* Write a scoped brief (options, criteria, weights agreed with the tech lead, constraints) and run
Research. Then open every citation behind a number or a compliance claim. Build the matrix with code, and
run a sensitivity check and a pre-mortem.
*Failure mode:* Research quotes "SOC 2 Type II certified" from a comparison blog. The vendor's trust page
shows Type I only. You find out at security review, after the recommendation was circulated.

**2. Pre-sales account research**
*Situation:* Before a discovery call, you need a brief on a prospect.
*Right move:* Research public material only (annual report, press releases, job postings, tech blog), with
sources and dates. Don't paste notes from your CRM about the deal's size or the champion's internal politics.
*Failure mode:* the deal value and the competitor you're displacing end up in a research prompt.

**3. Regulatory scan**
*Situation:* Legal asks which upcoming EU rules affect an AI feature.
*Right move:* Use primary sources only (official journal, regulator guidance), with publication and
effective dates. Mark every interpretation as interpretation. Legal owns the conclusion.
*Failure mode:* a summary of a summary states an application date that changed in the final text.

**4. Executive briefing**
*Situation:* The CTO wants one page on whether to adopt a new cloud region.
*Right move:* Summary first, with the trade-off in one sentence and the "what would have to be true"
conditions per option. Then the evidence table and three open questions with owners.
*Failure mode:* a two-page narrative ending in "we recommend X", with no conditions, no sources table and no
named owner. The CTO can't tell what would change the answer.

**5. Vendor selection with a seeded error**
*Situation:* A colleague's research summary is ready for the steering committee.
*Right move:* Before it goes, sample the citations behind every number and claim the decision turns
on. Find the one that doesn't say what's claimed. Fix it, then add a verification note (M8.2).

## Pitfalls

| Anti-pattern | Why it bites | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Treating "Claude searched it" as "it's verified" | Search ranks relevance, not truth | Open and check the sources behind high-risk claims |
| Reading the prose and skimming the citations | The prose is fluent whatever the evidence | Review the evidence table; check citations |
| Unscoped question ("research feature-flag tools") | Generic report, unusable for the decision | Brief: decision, options, criteria, constraints, horizon |
| Weights chosen after scoring | Rationalises a preferred answer | Agree the weights with the decision-maker first |
| Reporting a 0.05 lead as a winner | False precision on judgement inputs | Report ties; run a sensitivity check |
| Averaging conflicting sources | Hides which is right | Reconcile by date, scope and primacy, or log an open question |
| Client strategy in a research prompt | Confidentiality breach | Research the market; anonymise the client |
| Verdict with no conditions | The decision-maker can't judge it | Trade-offs plus "what would have to be true" |
| Research on mobile expecting Drive results | Connectors are skipped on mobile | Run it from web or desktop |

## Hands-on exercises

1. **Run Research on a technology-selection question.** Verify every cited source and score the report.
   **Done when:** each citation is marked ✅ supports / ◐ partly / ❌ doesn't support / 🔗 broken, and the
   report has a score out of 10 with the three biggest problems named.
2. **Weighted-criteria comparison of three tools, with sources.** Have a peer challenge two of your scores.
   **Done when:** the totals are computed with code, a sensitivity check shows whether the ranking flips, and
   both challenged scores are either defended with a source or changed.
3. **Pre-mortem a project decision** and produce the risk register.
   **Done when:** at least five failure stories, each with an early warning sign, a mitigation and a named owner.
4. **Find the three errors seeded in a provided research summary.**
   **Done when:** all three are found, each with the source that disproves it and the corrected sentence.
